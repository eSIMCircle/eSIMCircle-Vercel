SoftBank vs Docomo vs au for a Japan eSIM

How to think about SoftBank, Docomo and au when choosing a Japan eSIM, and what matters more than the network name.

Published September 28, 2026 · Updated October 11, 2026 · 3 minutes

What to prepare before the first travel day

This section keeps the focus on cost, convenience, and setup compared for Japan. Before choosing, picture the exact moments when being offline would be annoying: arrival, transport, check-in, route changes, or a payment screen. The right eSIM is the one that covers those moments without making you overbuy for days when hotel Wi-Fi will do most of the work.

The first hour after landing is when a missing connection costs the most: a map, a message to whoever is meeting you, a ride, and the address of your first stop. If the eSIM is installed beforehand, all four are available the moment you switch it on.

Keep large app updates, cloud backups and long uploads for Wi-Fi. They are the quiet drain on any allowance, because phones start them the moment they see a connection.

Roaming versus prepaid control

Set up the eSIM while you still have steady Wi-Fi and time to read the prompts. Save the QR email, name the travel line clearly, select it for mobile data, and turn on roaming for that line if the instructions require it. If service does not appear immediately, restart the phone and wait for network registration before deleting anything.

Keep your home SIM in the phone for calls and bank messages, and let the eSIM carry the data. Two lines in one phone is normal, and the settings that matter are which line handles mobile data and whether roaming is on for the eSIM line.

Plan the installation for a day or two before you fly. That is early enough to sort out a problem, and late enough that the QR email is still easy to find.

Pocket Wi-Fi versus individual phones

Roaming wins on convenience, but the cost may be hard to predict. A physical SIM can work, but it may mean finding a counter and handling a card swap. Pocket Wi-Fi can help a group, yet it adds charging and return logistics. An eSIM is the option you can prepare before the trip, which is why setup timing matters.

When a phone shows no mobile data after landing, check the settings before blaming the network. Confirm the eSIM is selected as the line for mobile data and that data roaming is on for that line. Then give it a few minutes, restart the phone, and check the selection again.

A failed installation is cheap to fix at home and expensive in an arrivals hall. Do it while Wi-Fi is stable, keep the QR email until the trip is over, and leave the profile on the phone unless support tells you to remove it.

What to check before choosing a travel data plan

Mobile data disappears through ordinary actions: map refreshes, ride confirmations, translation, ticket screens, restaurant searches, and messages to the people waiting for you. For cost, convenience, and setup compared, keep large updates, backups, and streaming on Wi-Fi. If a laptop hotspot or family sharing is part of the trip, choose more daily headroom than a maps-only traveler would need.

If you cannot decide between two sizes, take the smaller one and keep the option to top up. Every plan can be topped up with more data or extended with more days while it is active, so a first guess is allowed to be wrong.

Sharing a connection changes the arithmetic. Every device connected through your phone draws on the same allowance, including operating system updates and sync you did not ask for. Whether sharing is allowed at all depends on the plan's hotspot terms, which are listed on the plan page.

Small travel habits that make the plan last longer

A few quiet habits protect the plan. Download offline maps for long transfers, keep booking screenshots where you can reach them, pause app updates on mobile data, and save cloud backups for Wi-Fi. If the day includes hotspot work, treat that as a separate data budget rather than a small add-on.

An eSIM is not a substitute for preparation. Save the address of your first stop, your tickets and an offline map before you leave, so a gap in signal costs minutes and not the afternoon.

Over weeks, habit matters more than any one day. Moving large downloads and backups to Wi-Fi saves more data than any change of plan.

The Japan plans

As listed when these notes were last updated. The plan page keeps the current prices and terms, and any plan can be topped up or extended while it runs.

The list for Japan has 27 plans: 11 priced per day and 16 with a fixed allowance. Daily plans run from 1.89 to 13.55 USD, with allowances from 500MB to 10GB per day. Fixed plans run from 3.99 to 45.99 USD, with validity of 7, 15 or 30 days.

Japan eSIM 20GB 30 Days is both the longest fixed plan and the cheapest per gigabyte. It works out to about 1.08 USD per GB and 0.72 USD per day over 30 days.

Validity comes in steps, and trips do not. A trip of 8 days is one day longer than the 7-day plans, so the next fit is the 15-day plans. Counting arrival and departure days decides which step you need.

On the daily side, ten days at the smallest allowance (500MB per day) comes to 18.90 USD, and ten days at the largest (10GB per day) to 135.50 USD, if a daily plan is bought for each day.
